**Theme 1 — Late awareness of prolonged sitting and physical strain****
****(merges: sitting too long, eye strain, forgotten hydration, posture/stretch)**

All four are one root cause — focused work suppresses the body's internal signal until it's already a problem — surfacing in different body systems (back, eyes, hydration, posture).
This merges the original sitting-awareness and structured-break lines because both describe the same underlying gap: the person needs an earlier cue and a small action, not a complicated exercise programme.

*"I got headache & even pain in upper back while sitting continuously that day" — Sushant. "My eyes feels like warmest part of the body" — Karishma. "I check my bottle in the evening and realise I didn't drink" — Triveni.*

**Theme 2 — Work context makes breaks difficult to execute****
****(****was: "when I'm on calls, it's not my call to get up")****
**
Structurally different from Theme 1. This isn't about failing to notice the need, it's about being unable to act on a need that is already known, because the person is not in control of their own schedule in that moment. Meetings, deadlines, release work, and deep-focus periods make it socially or practically difficult to get up. The opportunity is to make the right action fit the work moment.

*"When you're on continuous calls back-to-back, it's not your call to get up" — Karishma, original interview.*

**Theme 3 — Break support must be comfortable, not controlling****
****(****merges: "feels like a manager checking on me" + "I know what I should do, but can't follow through consistently")****
**

Regular or visible reminders can feel like a manager checking progress, become annoying during busy periods, or get tuned out. This is not the primary problem; it is a design constraint on the solution. Nudges must be sparse, supportive, discreet, and easy to dismiss or defer.

*"If app which is already there have regular notification **pattern,** then it seems like a manager checking my progress" — Sushant.**

**Why does this happen?****
****
**Because desk workers become absorbed in focused work, calls, or deadlines and do not monitor elapsed sitting time or early body signals.

**Why does that happen?****
****
**Because work attention is directed toward the task, while the body’s warning signals are weak,
delayed, or easy to dismiss; existing reminders are not necessarily present or trusted.

**Why does that happen?****
****
**Because the workday has protected focus windows and unpredictable interruptions, so a generic reminder is either absent, mistimed, or feels like supervision. The person needs a cue that arrives at a workable moment and suggests a very small action.

**When does the pain happen?****
****
**During extended continuous desk sessions—often after roughly 60–90 minutes of sitting, especially during deep work, back-to-back meetings, or release work. The opportunity is therefore a moment-specific intervention: before or around the one-hour mark, unless the person is in a protected focus or meeting window.

Told the concept upfront that she resists prompts: "I take breaks when I feel like it, instead of waiting for some application to tell me that break Lelo" — and the data matched the words: compliance fell from 80% (Day 1) to 0% by Day 3, and nudges were deliberately stopped on Day 4 ("Didn't send any nudge further") in direct response to her disengagement.

She did not respond to the exit questionnaire at all — itself consistent with her stated preference to not be chased for check-ins.

**This is not a failed participant — it is a clean, real-world confirmation of the interview-stage warning that a fixed-cadence, prescriptive nudge will be actively rejected by some users, and that the product needs a lower-frequency or self-reporting mode.**

I sometimes keep my phone away unconsciously."** - ** ****This reframes her low compliance: it was not disinterest, it was a channel/delivery constraint (phone not ******on hand******), which a background app would solve in a way a WhatsApp message cannot.***

## **What This Test Validates**

Theme 1 (Delayed Bodily Awareness) is real and actionable — 3 of 6 participants sustained 80%+ compliance across 4 full days of real nudges, with no complaints about the category of nudge itself.

Theme 2 (Context Lock-Out) is not a hypothesis — it happened, twice, to the same person, for the same stated reason, and directly forced a live design change (the seated/on-call fallback) that then worked.

Theme 3 (Autonomy & Trust) is the constraint most likely to break the product if ignored — one participant's compliance predictably collapsed to 0% exactly as she warned it would, and another's highest possible satisfaction rating came with the lowest completion, showing frequency and perceived value can move independently.

Engagement is workload-sensitive, not just nudge-sensitive — the sharpest dip (Day 3, 36%) lines up with two independently reported workload spikes, not with "nudge fatigue" alone.

The average usefulness rating across all who responded to the exit survey was 4.2 / 5 — high, despite compliance varying from 19% to 95% — reinforcing that even people who didn't act on most nudges still found the concept valuable.
